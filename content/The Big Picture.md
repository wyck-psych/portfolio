Many questions in research and industry are answered using **behavioral measures**. Behavioral measures are a general term that includes psychological and educational tests, questionnaires, interviews, performances, or any other method aimed at systematically capturing and representing behavior as data. The data gathered using behavioral measures can be used as evidence to support inferences. For example, educators use standardized tests to evaluate student progress, political scientists use polls to understand the opinions of populations, user experience (UX) researchers use interviews and surveys to understand how people interact with products, and hiring committees use interviews to help them choose the best candidates for employment. 

Behavioral measures are often administered using **online platforms**. Compared to other methods of administration, online platforms are more convenient, cost effective, and allow for individuals in distant geographical areas to be involved. For example, researchers build and host questionnaires using platforms like Qualtrics, educators assign homework and assessments using learning management systems (LMS) and computer-based testing (CBT) platforms, and students can use online courses and learning apps to help them practice new skills. 

A benefit with using online platforms (computer-based platforms more broadly) for administering behavioral measures is that they can collect **process data** (a.k.a. paradata or log data) that can provide a more detailed picture of the response process. Many forms of process data can be collected "for free" as the participant completes the measure. These include response times, mouse-tracking, keyboard-tracking, and other event data. Some other forms of process data require more specialized hardware and have more limited use cases. These include eye-tracking, brain activity, and physiological markers. Process data represent a promising frontier for improving the validity and reliability of measurement as well as the predictive performance of machine learning models. However, when and how process data is useful is still an open question. 

A problem with using online platforms for administering behavioral measures is that they tend to be completed in less controlled conditions, which increases **measurement error** as there are more sources of construct-irrelevant variance. For example, participants may complete behavioral measures in distracting environments, multitask, or exhibit aberrant behaviors which are less common in controlled settings, such as careless / insufficient-effort responding, rapid guessing, cheating, or AI use. This leads to reduced confidence in the use of such data as evidence. 

Individuals are very likely to use AI tools to help them complete online behavioral measures. It is helpful to distinguish two major types of AI-related response behaviors: **AI-assisted responding** and **AI-agent responding**. Participants engage in AI-assisted responding when they initiate the measure themselves, utilize basic AI tools (e.g. copy/paste into a chatbot or a browser extension) to help them create a response, types or pastes the response, and submits the completed measure themselves. In contrast, AI-agent responding involves an AI agent which initiates, processes, and submits the measure with little to no interaction by the participant. Contemporary agentic AI workflows typically involve multimodal LLMs, which can process images along with text, and tools which allow it to interact with online platforms, access files stored on the participants computer, and run code on the participant's computer. 

Both AI-assisted responding and AI-agent responding are **multidimensional concepts** whose instances can vary within themselves across several dimensions. For example, AI-assisted responses can reflect varying levels of participant engagement; some participants may use AI tools critically and collaboratively while creating a response, while other participants may copy/paste with minimal effort. Furthermore, the behavior of AI-agents can vary depending on the details of the workflow. Specifically, AI-agents are often designed to simulate respondent personas, where responses are generated to be consistent with demographic information and contextually appropriate (including accurately representing the knowledge and cognitive abilities of the persona). 

**Researchers are beginning to document these emerging problems**. Currently, AI-assisted responding is very common, threatens the validity of research and other practices which rely on "genuine" responding (e.g. homework and assessments), and especially threatens open-ended, longer, and more cognitive load-inducing items. AI-assisted responding is expected to be a lingering issue for data collected from behavioral measures administered using online platforms. Currently, AI-agent responding represents a rare but potential threat [(Zhang et al., 2026)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2606460123). The tools for building powerful agentic workflows are publicly available and are expected to become more widely adopted in the near future. Moreover, agentic AI workflows open up the possibility of large-scale fraud. For example, an AI agent can complete a survey paying on average $1.50 for $0.05 worth of computing power [(Westwood, 2025)](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2518075122), providing a powerful financial incentive to develop these tools. 

An important related issue is **synthetic data** (i.e., simulated data, fabricated data, or fake data). Synthetic data are responses which are generated to have useful properties, such as mimicking real data distributions. Researchers often use synthetic data for legitimate purposes, such as testing their methods via simulations. However, synthetic data can also be used for fraudulent purposes when they are presented as being genuine responses. For example, a researcher might blend real and synthetic data to artificially inflate sample sizes or avoid collecting costly data, a corporation might use synthetic data to provide evidence for a social program having favorable outcomes, and an employee might use synthetic data to falsify records. Detecting the fraudulent use of synthetic data is an important scientific and industrial problem. 

### **The Problem**

**How can we detect AI-assisted responding and AI-agent responding in online platforms? Additionally, how useful are process data and ML/DL methods for detection?**

However, I feel confident that the problem is ready to be answered because I think that the tools are there. Process data is really promising and heavily underutilized, and ML/DL methods are a great tool for the job. This sentiment is shared by the proponents of [[Computational Psychometrics]] [(von Davier et al., 2021)](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-74394-9), who seek to analyze many varieties of process data using techniques from data mining and machine learning to solve measurement problems in education and psychology. This is a relatively focused research area involving mainly psychometricians, but there is enough research for an edited volume. Additionally, there are at least several related literatures that I can draw from to answer this question. The ML/DL methods literature is extensive, including a large amount of recent research specifically applying them in psychometric contexts. The psychometrics and test security literatures include methods for analyzing additional data types, analyzing person-fit, and detecting cheating and other aberrant response behaviors. Furthermore, this problem has real world importance right now; crowdsourcing platforms like Prolific and CloudResearch have developed their own proprietary AI detection systems and employ teams who work to prevent AI contamination specifically. See [[Industry Work on the Problem]] for more details. All of this gives me confidence that the problem is a good one to work on. The problem will be refined further as I think about how a detector works conceptually and what data and methods are involved.*
