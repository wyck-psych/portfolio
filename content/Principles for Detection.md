
**First, all detectors utilize some kind of dataset with observed variables as inputs**. Often, detection works with tabular data containing rows of observations and columns of features. Many process data features can be captured as time-stamped event data, which is more difficult to work with. Detection methods often make specific assumptions about the data, such as whether it is labeled and how much contamination (i.e., the percentage of observations which are anomalies) there is. 

**Second, all detectors utilize some kind of systematic rule/method for identifying assigning anomaly status to observations**. These rules can be based on psychometric or machine learning models. For example, [[Person-Fit Analysis]] in psychometrics utilizes the patterns of item responses to determine the extent to which observations are consistent with a proposed measurement model. Machine learning detection models often produce numerical scores that represent the degree to which the observation is anomalous. A threshold is used on these scores to assign anomaly status to observations, and the performance of the model is evaluated across a range of threshold values (See [[Receiver Operating Characteristic (ROC) Curves]]). 

**Third, all detectors utilize some concept of an observation being "genuine" or anomalous**. Some detectors are designed with the intention of making minimal theoretical assumptions (i.e., more data-driven, nonparametric, or exploratory), which leads to broader applicability and the potential to identify previously unknown forms of anomalies. Other detectors are designed with specific forms of anomalies in mind, such as detecting examinees using pre-knowledge cheating in exams and identifying the compromised items. Detection may also involve multiple kinds of genuine or anomalous observations. For example, a detector for AI-related behavior in online platforms may want to identify multiple forms of AI-assisted, AI-agent, and genuine responding. **Fourth, detection can be thought of in terms of joint distributions**. Whichever features we choose to use, they will form joint probability distributions in which certain combinations of features are more common than others. Some joint distribution patterns are associated with genuine or anomalous responding. Thus, the task of building a detector can be thought of as choosing/engineering features which will lead to the most distinguishability between genuine and anomalous joint distributions, which then leads to the most accurate and reliable detection. The difficulty is that the joint distributions in observed data contain a significant amount of both construct-relevant variance (i.e., real variation in genuine or anomalous response behaviors) and construct-irrelevant variance (i.e., noise or measurement error).
